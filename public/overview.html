When an AI reflects your feelings back, do you notice them more?

Affective Echoes runs live video conversations between a participant and Alex, an AI partner. It estimates how the participant feels from their face, voice and words, then shows that estimate back in one of three ways. The study asks which way helps people notice their own emotional changes.

One session, about 28 minutes in the app

Each participant gets one feedback condition for the whole session and meets Anger and Pride in a counterbalanced order. Calm stretches before and after each style give clear changes to notice.

  1. 1 min

    Rest

    Sitting quietly with a fixation cross, while EmotiBit and EEG record a baseline.

  2. 2 min

    Practice

    A neutral warm-up conversation, so the participant learns how talking with Alex works.

  3. 2 × 12 min

    Two emotion parts

    Each part includes a 1-minute neutral baseline, a 10-minute Anger or Pride conversation with several topics, and a 1-minute neutral recovery.

  4. every 1–2 min

    Check-ins

    During each emotional stretch, a few sliders ask how the participant feels. The conversation pauses while they answer.

  5. at the end

    Debrief

    The participant learns that Alex's critical or flattering replies were scripted, not a real judgment of them.

What's inside

Everything runs on the lab computer: a small Python server and a browser page. Video and affect estimates stay on that computer. Two parts use the internet: Chrome's speech recognition, and the language model if it's a cloud service rather than a local one.

Reads three signals at once

Facial expression from the webcam, loudness and pitch from the microphone, and the words the participant says. They're combined twice a second into one estimate of pleasantness and activation.

Three ways Alex responds

Polite turn-taking

Alex waits for 2.5 seconds of silence before treating an answer as finished, and stops mid-sentence to listen if the participant starts talking.

Designed not to become part of the experiment

In an emotion study, the interface is itself a stimulus. These choices keep it out of the way.

Colour carries emotion in one place only
Participant screens stay cool and neutral. The only colour that responds to feelings is the subtitle window in the other-mediated condition.
Nothing breathes on the rest screens
Pulsing or breathing animations can pace breathing and shift heart-rate variability, so rest and break screens are completely still.
Every participant sees the same screen
The app ignores the computer's dark mode and other display preferences that would change the stimulus.
A face that can't make expressions
Alex is a set of rings that ripple only while speaking, so no facial expression can sway how the participant feels.
